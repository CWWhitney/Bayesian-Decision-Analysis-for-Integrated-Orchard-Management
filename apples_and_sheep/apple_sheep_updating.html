Problem Description: Bayesian Decision Analysis for Integrated Orchard Management

Background

You are considering introducing sheep into your apple orchard, a practice known as “agroforestry” or “integrated farming.” This approach promises multiple benefits but also carries significant uncertainties. The decision involves complex trade-offs between costs, benefits, and risks that are difficult to quantify with traditional analysis.

The Decision Context

Current Situation

  • Apple orchard: Established operation with known annual revenues
  • Opportunity: Introduce sheep for wool/meat production while benefiting orchard health
  • Challenge: Multiple uncertain factors make traditional cost-benefit analysis inadequate

Key Uncertain Variables

Costs (Annual)

  • Initial investment: Fencing, watering systems, sheep purchase
  • Operating costs: Feed supplements, veterinary care, labor
  • Potential losses: Sheep damage to trees, increased management complexity

Benefits

  • Direct revenue: Wool, meat, lamb sales
  • Orchard benefits: Natural fertilization, weed control, reduced mowing costs
  • Ecosystem services: Improved soil health, biodiversity enhancement

Risks

  • Animal health risks: Disease outbreaks, predation losses
  • Market risks: Fluctuating wool/meat prices
  • Orchard risks: Tree damage, soil compaction, regulatory changes

The Analytical Challenge

Problem 1: Parameter Uncertainty

You have initial estimates with 90% confidence intervals, but these ranges are wide: - Costs: $5,000 - $15,000 annually - Benefits: $8,000 - $20,000 annually
- Failure probability: 10% - 30%

Problem 2: Incorporating New Information

You’ve gathered observational data from another farm, but: - Their operation isn’t identical to yours - Their results may not directly translate to your context - You want to use this information without over-relying on it

Problem 3: Decision Complexity

The decision requires answering: 1. What is the expected net benefit? 2. What is the probability of positive returns? 3. What are the potential downside risks? 4. How robust is the decision to changing assumptions?

Bayesian Monte Carlo Approach

Why Bayesian Methods?

  • Incorporate prior knowledge: Your expert judgment about reasonable ranges
  • Update with new evidence: Observations from other farms inform but don’t dominate
  • Quantify uncertainty: Results include probability distributions, not just point estimates

Why Monte Carlo Simulation?

  • Propagate uncertainty: Track how input uncertainties affect output uncertainty
  • Scenario analysis: Explore thousands of possible futures
  • Risk assessment: Quantify probabilities of different outcomes

Research Questions

This analysis addresses:

  1. Decision Support: Should you introduce sheep given current information?
  2. Value of Information: How much would perfect information about key parameters be worth?
  3. Sensitivity Analysis: Which parameters most strongly influence the decision?
  4. Risk Management: What are the potential downside scenarios and their probabilities?

Expected Outcomes

The analysis will provide: - Probability distribution of net annual benefits - Probability of economic success/failure - Identification of key risk drivers - Guidance on whether to proceed, and under what conditions

Practical Significance

This approach demonstrates how farmers can make better decisions under uncertainty by: - Systematically incorporating different types of information - Quantifying risks rather than ignoring them - Making transparent, defensible decisions - Planning for multiple possible futures

The methodology is transferable to other agricultural decisions involving: - New technology adoption - Crop diversification
- Sustainable practice implementation - Climate adaptation strategies

We provide a simple R script that demonstrates how to use Monte Carlo simulation with Bayesian updating for farm decision:

Simple Bayesian Monte Carlo for Farm Decision Analysis

Introducing sheep in apple orchard

set.seed(123) # For reproducible results

Define prior distributions (90% confidence intervals)

Using normal distributions for simplicity

# Prior parameters (mean and sd based on 90% CI)
# Assuming 90% CI is mean ± 1.645*sd
calculate_prior <- function(lower, upper) {
  mean_val <- (lower + upper) / 2
  sd_val <- (upper - lower) / (2 * 1.645) # 1.645 for 90% CI
  return(list(mean = mean_val, sd = sd_val))
}

Prior distributions for costs, benefits, and risks

cost_prior <- calculate_prior(lower = 5000, upper = 15000)    # Annual costs ($)
benefit_prior <- calculate_prior(lower = 8000, upper = 20000) # Annual benefits ($)
risk_prior <- calculate_prior(lower = 0.1, upper = 0.3)       # Probability of failure

New observations from other farm

observations <- list(
  wool_price_obs = 12.5,    # $/kg observed wool price
  fertility_gain_obs = 0.15, # Observed fertility improvement
  risk_obs = 0.18           # Observed failure rate
)

Bayesian updating function

bayesian_update <- function(prior_mean, prior_sd, observed_value, obs_sd = 0.1) {
  # Simple conjugate prior update for normal distribution
  prior_precision <- 1 / (prior_sd^2)
  obs_precision <- 1 / (obs_sd^2)
  
  posterior_mean <- (prior_precision * prior_mean + obs_precision * observed_value) / 
                    (prior_precision + obs_precision)
  posterior_sd <- sqrt(1 / (prior_precision + obs_precision))
  
  return(list(mean = posterior_mean, sd = posterior_sd))
}

Update priors with observations (with some uncertainty in observations)

cost_posterior <- bayesian_update(cost_prior$mean, cost_prior$sd, 
                                 observations$wool_price_obs * 400) # Convert to annual cost
benefit_posterior <- bayesian_update(benefit_prior$mean, benefit_prior$sd,
                                    observations$fertility_gain_obs * 100000) # Convert to monetary value
risk_posterior <- bayesian_update(risk_prior$mean, risk_prior$sd,
                                 observations$risk_obs)

Monte Carlo simulation

n_simulations <- 10000

# Sample from posterior distributions
costs_sim <- rnorm(n_simulations, cost_posterior$mean, cost_posterior$sd)
benefits_sim <- rnorm(n_simulations, benefit_posterior$mean, benefit_posterior$sd)
risks_sim <- rnorm(n_simulations, risk_posterior$mean, risk_posterior$sd)

# Ensure risks are between 0 and 1
risks_sim <- pmax(0, pmin(1, risks_sim))

# Calculate net benefits (accounting for risk)
net_benefits <- (benefits_sim - costs_sim) * (1 - risks_sim)

# Decision analysis results
results <- list(
  expected_net_benefit = mean(net_benefits),
  probability_positive = mean(net_benefits > 0),
  confidence_interval = quantile(net_benefits, c(0.05, 0.95)),
  risk_exposure = mean(net_benefits < -5000) # Probability of significant loss
)
